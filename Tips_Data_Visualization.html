Data Visualization with Matplotlib & Seaborn¶

Project 02 | Data Science & AI | InternCircle

Goal: Create a set of clear, honest charts from a real dataset (restaurant tips): a histogram, box plot, bar charts, scatter plot and a correlation heatmap. Every chart has a finding-style title, labelled axes with units, and a one-line takeaway.

Tools: Python, pandas, matplotlib, seaborn

1. My Questions¶

  1. Q1: How are restaurant bills distributed?
  2. Q2: Do tips differ by day of the week?
  3. Q3: Does a bigger bill mean a bigger tip?
  4. Q4: Which numbers move together?

2. Matching Charts to Questions¶

Question Chart Why this chart
Q1 Bill distribution Histogram Shows the shape of one number
Q2 Tips by day Box plot and bar charts Box plot compares a number across groups; bars show the averages from a zero baseline
Q3 Bill vs tip Scatter plot Shows the relationship between two numbers
Q4 What moves together Correlation heatmap Shows every pair of numbers at once

4. Colour Palette and Style¶

I use one colour-blind-friendly palette (blue and orange, no red/green together). Colour is used to mean something: orange = Lunch, blue = Dinner on the scatter plot. Other charts use the same blue so the look stays consistent.

5. Chart Functions¶

I write each chart as a small function so I can draw it on its own and then reuse it in the final 2 x 2 dashboard without repeating code.

In [4]:
def draw_hist(ax):
    sns.histplot(tips["total_bill"], bins=20, color=BLUE, ax=ax)
    ax.set(title="Most bills are between \$10 and \$25",
           xlabel="Total bill (USD)", ylabel="Number of meals")

def draw_box(ax):
    sns.boxplot(data=tips, x="day", y="tip", order=DAY_ORDER, color=BLUE, ax=ax)
    ax.set(title="Sunday tips are highest, Thursday lowest",
           xlabel="Day of week", ylabel="Tip (USD)")
    ax.set_ylim(bottom=0)

def draw_scatter(ax):
    sns.scatterplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip", hue="time",
                    palette=TIME_COLORS, alpha=0.8, ax=ax)
    ax.set(title="Bigger bills bring bigger tips",
           xlabel="Total bill (USD)", ylabel="Tip (USD)")
    ax.set_xlim(left=0)
    ax.set_ylim(bottom=0)
    ax.legend(title="Meal time")

def draw_heatmap(ax):
    corr = tips[["total_bill", "tip", "size"]].corr()
    sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="Blues", vmin=0, vmax=1,
                square=True, cbar_kws={"label": "Correlation (r)"}, ax=ax)
    ax.set_title("Bill and tip move together most (r = 0.68)")

Takeaway: Yes, tips rise with the bill (r = 0.68), and dinner meals (blue) tend to have both larger bills and larger tips than lunch (orange); the spread also widens for bigger bills, so the tip is not exactly fixed.

Q4. Which numbers move together? (Correlation heatmap)¶

In [9]:
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 5.5))
draw_heatmap(ax)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

How to read it: each square is the correlation (r) between two numbers. 1.00 means they move perfectly together and 0 means no straight-line link. The diagonal is always 1.00 because each number is compared with itself.

Takeaway: The strongest relationship is between total bill and tip (r = 0.68), followed by bill and party size (0.60) and tip and party size (0.49). Bigger parties spend more, and bigger bills mean bigger tips.

8. Written Takeaways (summary)¶

  1. Bill distribution: About 71% of bills are between $10 and $25, with a median of about $18.
  2. Tips by day: Sunday has the highest median tip ($3.15) and Thursday the lowest ($2.30), but tip percentage is about the same every day (15% to 17%).
  3. Bill vs tip: Bigger bills bring bigger tips (r = 0.68).
  4. What moves together: Bill and tip have the strongest relationship (r = 0.68), then bill and party size (0.60).

9. Review for Honesty¶

  • Zero baselines: histogram counts, bar charts and the box plot / scatter y-axes all start at zero, so nothing exaggerates a difference.
  • Nothing is cut off: plt.tight_layout() is used on every figure.
  • Colour: one blue/orange colour-blind-friendly palette; no red and green together; no 3D or pie charts.
  • Titles state the finding, and every axis has a label with units (USD, %).
  • Limits: the data has only 244 meals (Friday has just 19). Correlation does not prove cause: a bigger bill does not cause a bigger tip, they are linked because tips are usually a share of the bill. The correlation (r) only measures straight-line links.
